# What Makes This Not D&D
### Three systems D&D can't do: the 2:47 Protocol, Static, and Transmissions · ⚠️ GM EYES ONLY

D&D is a game about *power* — you get stronger until the dragon stops being
scary. PORCHLIGHT is a game about *identity under pressure* — the invasion's
real weapon isn't the beam, it's the incentive to be a little less yourself
every day. These three systems make that playable.

---

## 1. THE 2:47 PROTOCOL (the haunted hour)

Once per session — when tension needs a spike, or when the player chooses to
be awake for it — **it becomes 2:47 AM.** This is a *mode*, not a scene: the
app itself goes dark (the button lives in the GM control room; it flips the
shared Discord window too, hum and all), and different physics apply until
you end it:

- **Clones sync.** Most Neighborly Units stand motionless, mid-gesture,
  receiving. Sneaking past a frozen clone is automatic. *Being somewhere you
  shouldn't be when the sync ends is the horror movie.*
- **The network is louder but blinder.** All Weird checks at advantage; all
  Charm vs. clones impossible (there's nobody home).
- **Everything alien is visible.** Glorp trails glow faintly without a
  blacklight. The tower hums audibly anywhere in town.
- **The cost:** anything the player does at 2:47 is *noticed by the network
  as a pattern* — two consecutive nights of 2:47 activity = 👁 +1 each night
  after the first. The hour is powerful and it is habit-forming, and the
  Collective loves nothing more than a habit.

**When the mode starts, draw a night event** (the app deals one automatically;
d12 deck below). The event is the hour's weather — sometimes an opening,
sometimes a horror, sometimes a gift.

### The Night Deck (d12)

1. **The Sync.** Every clone in town freezes mid-step for 66 seconds. Any
   door is enterable. The countdown restarts exactly when the player is
   furthest from the exit.
2. **The Turning.** Every gnome rotates to face the water tower. All of them.
   Even the one in the kitchen nobody has mentioned yet.
3. **The Delivery.** A FRSHMRT truck idles outside a house that still has
   people in it. The driver waits. Checks a list. Waits.
4. **Two Louder.** The Lawn Frequency rises by exactly two houses' worth.
   By morning, two more porch lights never turn off.
5. **The Dog Knows.** Biscuit at the window: "You need to see this. Bring
   the steak. Not for the mission. For me."
6. **Broadcast Night.** Every TV in every replaced house turns on at once —
   the same 1950s sitcom, laugh track two seconds late, blue light flickering
   down the whole street in unison.
7. **The Taking.** The beam comes for someone tonight — someone whose name
   the player knows. Intervene (loud, heroic, 👁 +2) or witness (a clue,
   and the guilt). There is no third option and they'll ask.
8. **After Hours.** Duke's light is on. Marla is pouring coffee for a
   stranger in pajamas who doesn't know how they got there. The stranger's
   slippers are wet with reservoir water.
9. **One Ring.** The player's phone rings once. Unknown number. Answered:
   66 seconds of suburb ambience — sprinkler, mower, laugh track — then,
   quietly: "WE KNOW YOU'RE AWAKE."
10. **The Pattern.** The sprinklers run in sequence. From a roof, it's
    readable: an arrow. Or a countdown. Petunia the drone could say which.
11. **The Silence.** Nothing happens. The first silent 2:47 in months — and
    that's the most frightening one yet. (GM truth: the network was busy
    elsewhere. Something big just advanced a step.)
12. **The Old Pattern.** The player's own porch light flickers in a rhythm
    they *almost* remember — the one from '99. Weird DC 8: a fragment of
    Thread B surfaces (`story-bible.md` §2). The house remembers something
    they don't.

## 2. STATIC (the identity-erosion track — the anti-Weird meter)

**The pitch:** in D&D, using your powers makes you more yourself. In
Pinebrook, *passing as perfect* makes you less. Static (0–9, on the character
sheet) measures how much of the player has been quietly overwritten by the
broadcast. The town likes a Smooth person better. That's the trap.

**Static rises (+1 unless noted):**
- Using a Glorptech item's power in a scene (the Quirk is the tell — the tech
  is *teaching your hands its manners*)
- A flawless Normalcy performance — passing a check by being *perfect* rather
  than clever ("I give the HOA exactly the smile they want")
- **+2** accepting a gift from Crisp (the Offer's lemonade counts)
- Witnessing something no human should see, up close (GM's call, sparingly)

**Static falls:**
- **−1** each SUBLIME power use (nothing is less broadcast-appropriate)
- **−1** a *being known* moment: telling an ally something true and
  unflattering about yourself (once per session)
- **−2** doing your Session-Zero thing in public, badly, on purpose —
  imperfect and *yours*

**Thresholds:**
- **3 — FLICKER.** The player chooses one small habit their character has.
  It's gone. (Don't assign it — making them choose is the mechanic.)
  Allies pause mid-conversation around them, just for a second.
- **6 — THE REFLECTION WAVES FIRST.** The network can now predict them:
  first roll each scene at disadvantage *until they do something genuinely
  unpredictable* in that scene. (This turns "be weird" into tactics.)
- **9 — SMOOTH.** One step from the broadcast. In the Undermart, a pod has
  been freshly made up. The linens are crisp. The mint is on the pillow.
  It is warm. **If the campaign ends while Static is 9, the epilogue
  includes an empty chair at Duke's** — whatever else was won.

**Why it works:** Static gives the comedy stakes. Every joke about pickle
brine is also a question — *how much of yourself will you trade to fit in?* —
and the finale's Weirdness Gambit becomes the answer the whole campaign was
building: the town is saved by everything the player refused to smooth away.

## 3. TRANSMISSIONS (the campaign plays between sessions)

Built for exactly your table: one player, one GM, Discord. Between sessions,
queue **transmissions** in the app (GM control room → Transmissions): short
in-fiction messages that arrive with a 📻 badge the next time the player
opens the app. Opening one plays it as a cinematic card, hum and all —
timestamped, always, 2:47 AM.

Rules of the form: **short** (a voicemail, not a chapter), **concrete** (one
image, one hook), **never requires an answer** (it's dread, not homework),
one or two per week, tops.

### Ten ready to send (adapt freely)

1. "This is Wendell. It's 2:47. The frequency just — it's not a spike
   tonight. It's a REPLY. Somebody answered. Go back to sleep. Don't."
2. A photo of the PC's own porch, taken from the street, timestamped 2:47.
   Nothing is wrong with it. Nothing at all. It's a lovely porch.
3. "Hi hon, it's Diane! Gary wants your casserole dish back — the good one.
   Also — this is silly — do you remember what color Gary's eyes USED to be?
   No reason. Bring the dish Sunday!"
4. Biscuit (a series of texts, somehow): "STEAK." "the tunnel is longer than
   we thought." "STEAK?" "it goes under the school." "steak."
5. A Gazette clipping, tomorrow's date: "AREA RESIDENT SLEEPING WELL,
   NEIGHBORS CONFIRM." The player has not been sleeping well.
6. "Priya. Encrypted channel." (It's a normal text.) "Gnome 14 moved 40 feet
   last night. That's not patrol range. That's a JOURNEY. It's going
   somewhere. I'm going to follow it. Bring snacks to the usual place."
7. A voicemail of pure diner ambience: dishes, coffee pouring, Duke's bell —
   one ding, two dings, then a long, deliberate FURIOUS PARADIDDLE.
8. "THIS IS A COURTESY NOTICE FROM THE NEIGHBORHOOD IMPROVEMENT ASSOCIATION.
   YOUR LAWN HAS BEEN PRE-APPROVED. NO ACTION IS REQUIRED. WE ARE SO PLEASED.
   NO ACTION IS REQUIRED." (Sent twice. Exactly twice.)
9. From Marla: "Sweetie. The stranger in the pajamas came back. He asked for
   you by name. Your FULL name. I didn't tell him anything. He tipped in
   exact change and left the mint on the table."
10. 66 seconds of the hum, recorded. Nothing else. Near the end — faint,
    almost polite — something that might be a voice saying: "SOON, PLEASE."

---

**Table note:** all three systems share one design rule — they never take
control away from the player. The hour tempts, Static prices, transmissions
whisper. The player always chooses. That's the difference between horror
and dread, and dread is funnier.
